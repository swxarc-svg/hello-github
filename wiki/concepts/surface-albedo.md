## Definition

Surface albedo is the fraction of solar radiation reflected by a surface. Higher albedo surfaces (lighter, more reflective) absorb less heat, reducing surface temperature (Ts) and air temperature (Ta). However, in unshaded outdoor pedestrian environments, higher albedo surfaces also increase short-wave radiation reflected toward pedestrians, raising mean radiant temperature (MRT) — a critical counter-intuitive finding for urban design.

## Current state of research

**Key finding** (Ghoniem et al. 2025): The albedo–MRT trade-off:
- Albedo 0.30 vs 0.12: Ts reduced by **7 °C** at 13:00; Ta reduced by **1.5 °C** at 15:00
- But MRT *increased* by up to **6 °C** (from 51 °C to 56 °C at 9:00; from 70 °C to 72 °C at 13:00) due to shortwave upward reflection
- Net effect: UTCI rose from 45.8 °C to 46.9 °C ("very strong" → "extreme" heat stress) at 15:00 in unshaded conditions
- **With canopy shading**: UTCI declined 5–6.6 °C regardless of albedo — shading neutralizes albedo penalty

**Practical implication**: Cool pavement strategies (high albedo) must be paired with overhead shading to avoid worsening pedestrian thermal stress. Shading is the primary lever; material selection is secondary.

**Degradation**: Reflective pavements show albedo degradation over time due to dust and wear — maintenance protocols required.

## Key debates & contradictions

- **Cool city vs. hot pedestrian paradox**: urban-scale albedo increases reduce urban heat island but can worsen pedestrian-level comfort in unshaded spaces
- **Climate dependence**: The MRT penalty may vary with latitude, sun angle, and sky conditions; findings from Cairo (hot-arid) need testing in humid-hot/overcast contexts like Macau
- **Vegetation interaction**: Ghoniem 2025 excluded vegetation from baseline; real settings with trees may dampen the MRT penalty
